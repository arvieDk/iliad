After the Iliad

The Iliad ends with Hector’s funeral, not with the fall of Troy. The rest of the story was told in other poems, now mostly lost. Achilles dies soon after, shot by Paris with the help of Apollo, just as Hector foretold. Troy falls to the trick of the wooden horse, devised by Odysseus, whose long voyage home is the subject of the Odyssey.

What the Iliad gives us is a few weeks in the tenth year of the war, and one man’s anger, from its first spark to its last ember in a tent at night, where two enemies weep together.

The text

The scenes retell a condensed version of the story in plain modern English. Every quotation is taken word for word from Homer, The Iliad, translated by Samuel Butler (1898), revised by Timothy Power and Gregory Nagy, from the Perseus Digital Library, Tufts University, used under CC BY-SA 4.0. Omissions are marked with an ellipsis, and each quotation gives its book and line numbers.

The pictures

All the illustrations are drawn in code, in the manner of Greek vase painting: black figures on clay for scenes by day, clay-red figures on black for scenes by night. Sounds are made in the browser.
